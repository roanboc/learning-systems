Train a creature

Nervous systems · reward, dopamine and a brain that wires itself

A small creature with a Tissue Lab brain smells a cue and turns with two muscles. Nothing tells it what to do. Teach it like an animal: click the arena to put the smell somewhere, then press Good when it does what you hoped and Bad when it does not. Good is a click followed by a treat in its gut; Bad is a sting.

At first dopamine fires when the treat lands: a surprise. After a dozen or so Goods it fires at the click instead, and the treat no longer surprises. The click has become rewarding in itself, as in clicker training.

Learning

facing the smell (0 = at random, 1 = always) smell → turn toward smell → turn away

Wiring is the total strength of paths from each smell cell to the muscle that turns toward it, or away from it. Reward moves them apart. In test runs with the helper on, taught creatures found the smell 3 to 6 times as often as untrained ones in 5 of 6 tries.

What is going on

  • No built-in "good". Good means a treat arrives in the gut; Bad means damage. The brain only ever sees its senses and a dopamine signal.
  • Dopamine is surprise. Dopamine cells compare what the gut got with what they expected from what was heard, like midbrain dopamine neurons (Schultz 1997). Better than expected: a burst. Worse: a dip.
  • Dopamine picks which synapses change. A synapse whose input fired just before its cell did keeps a short trace. Dopamine turns that trace into a stronger or weaker synapse, so the actions just before a reward are the ones that stick.
  • The tissue stays alive. Neurons are still born, compete and die, and glia still prune unused synapses. A smell connection that goes unused for long can be pruned and the skill fades: use it or lose it.
