Colonies: sharers and cheats

The same coast, with colonies of cells and predators that swallow anything small. Here cells can make a helper molecule that frees food from the water for everyone nearby. Making it costs energy. What is real here, and what is simplified

Public goods. Many microbes release molecules that help every cell nearby: bacteria release siderophores to grab iron, yeast releases an enzyme that digests sugar outside the cell. The maker pays; any neighbour benefits. Cells that make none (cheats) arise by mutation and save the cost. Here the helper molecule turns raw molecules in the water into building blocks, which any cell at that spot can eat.

Why sharing survives. A sharer's help lands where it is. If its neighbours are its own daughters, the help goes to copies of the same gene, and sharing spreads. If neighbours are strangers, cheats get the same help for free and win. This is kin selection (Hamilton's rule: share when relatedness × benefit > cost) and spatial structure, two of Nowak's five rules for cooperation. Both are well supported by experiments with bacteria and yeast (Griffin, West and Buckling 2004; Gore and colleagues 2009; Allen, Gore and Nowak 2013).

Where the trait lives. In microbes, only in genes: one gene here says how much helper a cell makes. Real bacteria also switch production on only when enough neighbours are around (quorum sensing), which is still a gene circuit, not a nervous system. Animals add brains that decide in the moment and learn whom to trust.

What we found. See the guided lab and the README: with kin stuck together the helper gene holds and even rises from a cheating start; when daughters drift apart it falls. The change is slow, over many days.

Simplified. The helper is a made-up molecule, freed blocks come from raw molecules (nothing is created), one map cell is far bigger than a real cell, and genes are numbers, not DNA. Real cooperation also uses policing and partner choice, which are not here yet.
