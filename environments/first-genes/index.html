Timeline of life · Chapter 1

First genes: a protocell that makes itself

The same coast as Early Earth, now with protocells: bubbles holding copiers that do a job. Skin-makers turn raw molecules into oils for the bubble's own skin, block-makers make its building blocks, and plain copiers only copy, faster than the rest, like parasites. A protocell whose genes work grows and splits, and its genes are shared between the daughters by chance. Green protocells are rich in working genes, pink ones are full of parasites; a thick golden skin means the protocell makes most of itself.

Day 0 · 00:00
Compare worlds

Makes itself: the share of a protocell's skin and building blocks that its own genes made, rather than taken from the water, averaged over protocells. Working genes: skin-makers and block-makers as a share of all copiers inside protocells.

How this world works

Not real chemistry. The coast, sun, tides, vents and molecules are the ones from Early Earth. What is new is inside the bubbles.

Copiers with a job. A copier is a chain that helps make more of itself from building blocks. Here a few copiers also do something: skin-makers turn raw molecules from the water into oils that become the bubble's skin, and block-makers turn raw molecules and energy into building blocks inside. Both jobs cost the bubble energy. These copiers are the first genes: copied molecules whose work keeps their own protocell going. This is the RNA world idea, in which the first genes were RNA that both copied and catalysed (debated, but the leading working hypothesis).

Parasites. Working genes copy 10% slower than plain copiers, and now and then a copy comes out broken, as a plain copier. Inside one protocell, plain copiers always win. They do no work, so a protocell taken over by them can no longer make its skin; it shrinks and breaks up.

Reproduction and the stochastic corrector. A protocell splits in two when its skin has doubled. Each kind of gene is shared between the daughters by chance, molecule by molecule, so some daughters get more working genes than others. Daughters with more working genes grow and split sooner. That selection between protocells keeps working genes going against parasites (Szathmáry and Demeter 1987; well supported in models and RNA experiments). The sea has room for only so many protocells; when it is full, random ones break up.

Makes itself is a measure of closure: how much of the protocell is produced by its own network rather than taken from outside. It goes from 0 to 100% and can rise or fall, which is how researchers now use the old idea of autopoiesis (Moreno and Mossio 2015). Our number is a teaching proxy, not a measurement from nature.

Worlds to compare. Leaky skin: what genes make leaks into the water and helps every bubble nearby, so working genes no longer pay their own protocell. No skin-makers: protocells must take all their oils from the water. Linked genes: all genes are joined in one chain, a first chromosome, copied together and passed on together, at a cost in copying speed.

Autopoiesis along the timeline

Autopoiesis (Maturana and Varela, 1974) says a living thing is a network that keeps producing the parts that produce it, inside a boundary it makes itself. Today most researchers keep the core idea but treat it as a matter of degree, under the name organizational closure, and add what the original left out: evolution and reproduction. Here is how the idea shows up in each chapter of Gaia.

  1. Early Earth about 4.4 billion years agoBubbles form from vent oils, grow and split, but nothing inside them makes anything. Their order is kept by a flow of matter and energy, like a flame. No closure. Order from a flow is necessary for life, not sufficient (well supported).
  2. First genes before 4.2 billion years agoGenes make the skin and the blocks, and are kept inside the skin they make. Closure rises from zero and is passed on at each split. Metabolism, genes and a boundary that make each other possible: Gánti's chemoton (well supported as a model; how it happened on Earth is debated).
  3. First cells about 3.8 billion years agoCells eat, move toward food and divide, and every ability comes from their own genes. The last common ancestor of all life, about 4.2 billion years ago, already had a genome of about 2,600 proteins (Moody et al. 2024, debated).
  4. Colonies about 3.5 billion years agoA helper molecule feeds everyone nearby, and cheats use it without paying: the parasite problem again, one level up. Staying together with your family keeps sharing alive, as the skin does for genes (well supported).
  5. Bodies about 1.5 billion to 600 million years agoA body keeps itself going with cells that each do one job; only germ cells reproduce. Closure now spans many cells (debated as to how far the idea stretches).
  6. Brains about 540 million years agoCreatures keep themselves going by what they do: sensing, moving and learning. Enactivist researchers extend closure to behaviour, sensorimotor autonomy (Di Paolo, Buhrmann and Barandiaran 2017, debated).

How Gaia reproduces

Every chapter reproduces, each in its own way. Sex, two parents mixing their genes, comes in the Bodies chapter; its oldest clear fossil is the red alga Bangiomorpha, 1.047 billion years ago (Gibson et al. 2018, well supported).

When did this happen?

Before about 4.2 billion years ago. No gene has left a fossil. The last common ancestor of all life already had a full genetic system, and gene-tree dating puts it at about 4.2 billion years ago (4.09 to 4.33; Moody et al. 2024, debated: other studies give younger or older dates). Liquid oceans existed by 4.4 billion years ago, shown by zircon crystals from Jack Hills, Western Australia (Wilde et al. 2001, well supported). So genes began somewhere between the two.
