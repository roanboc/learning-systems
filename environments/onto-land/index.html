Onto land: body plans

The Ecosystems coast, about 430 to 375 million years ago. Every grazer's body is now a row of segments. Its trunk carries pairs of fins or legs, its tail a fin, and its genes say how many, how long and how sturdy. In water, slender bodies with fins swim fastest. On dry ground only legs walk, and only if they can hold the body up. Left: green mats grow on the damp ground the tide uncovers. Right: the same coast with nothing to eat on land. How a body works here

Segments. A body has 3 to 12 segments. Longer bodies are more streamlined and swim faster, but they weigh more on land.

Limbs. One gene sets the kind of limb for the whole trunk: none, fins or legs. A mutation that crosses from fins to legs changes every pair at once, as a mutation in a Hox gene can turn one kind of appendage into another. Other genes set the number of pairs, their length and how sturdy they are.

In water. Fins add a little thrust, legs add drag, and a tail fin pushes.

On dry ground. Only legs step out of the water. Walking speed depends on how well the legs hold the body up: pairs × sturdiness against the body's weight (its segments). A body its legs can't hold drags itself along.

Air. Out of water a creature dries out and loses energy every step. An air skin (one gene for a tougher skin and a first lung) slows that, and costs a little upkeep.

Hunters need water at least a metre deep, so the shallows and the shore are a refuge. Their shape evolves too and sets how fast they swim, but their body parts cost them nothing.

Was shaping more creative on land?

Only partly. Almost every animal body plan, and the first legs, arms, antennae and fins, appeared in the sea during the Cambrian explosion, about 539 to 515 million years ago well supported. Groups reach their widest range of shapes early in their history well supported.

The tools for shaping were ready before land. The same genes lay out fish fins, insect legs, worm paddles and sea-urchin tube feet (Hox genes along the body, Distal-less where an outgrowth grows), so their common ancestor already had them, in the sea well supported.

Legs began in water. Tiktaalik, 375 million years ago, was a fish with sturdy, wrist-like fins in shallow water. Early four-limbed animals such as Acanthostega had toes and still lived in water. Footprints on a tidal flat in Poland are about 395 million years old well supported.

Crossing to land did not widen the range of limb shapes: fins just before and limbs just after show a similar spread debated (one study).

Where the idea holds. Land rebuilt old parts for new jobs: fins into legs that carry weight, gill supports into ear bones and, later, wings. And land lineages split into new species faster, which is why most species today live on land well supported. So the sea invented the body plans, and land remodelled them and multiplied species debated as a summary of "more creative".

The systems idea: modularity. A body built from repeated parts can change by copying a part or switching what a whole region grows. That lets evolution try many shapes quickly debated in real animals, strong in models.

What we found

Six seeds, 10,000 steps (about 14 days) each, three coasts per seed: shore mats, the twin with nothing on land, and mats with no hunters. Averages over the last 2,000 steps, with the range across seeds. Details are in the README.

Shape follows place. With mats, legs spread to over a quarter of the grazers in 4 of 6 seeds; on the twin, in none. Legged grazers gathered on the shore, and their legs held more of their weight.

What did not happen. The sturdiness and air-skin genes rose on both coasts alike: they cost little, so they drift. Longer bodies did not carry sturdier legs. Hunters survived on only 2 of 6 seeds, on both coasts.

The open question. Disparity rose about tenfold on all three coasts and was not clearly higher with food on land. Here land changed which shapes won more than how varied they were, much like the fossil study of fins and limbs. Without hunters, legs spread on only 1 of 6 seeds, so hunters may help push grazers ashore (an early sign).

What is real here, and what is simplified

Grounded. Bodies built from repeated segments, one limb type per body region, weight support as the main problem on land, drying out, and food on land before animals: plants and microbial mats reached land before animals did, and the first land animals ate plants and debris.

Simplified. Three regions instead of real Hox domains, one gene for skin and lungs together, mats that need only sunlight, and costs and speeds chosen so the trade-offs show. Drying out is gentler here than on the other coasts (damp ground). Hunters pay nothing for their shape: when they did, they died out in four of five test seeds. Even so they survive on only two of six seeds (Ecosystems: three of four). The page runs seed 1, where they survive.
