Bodies: the first muscles

The same coast, with bodies of stuck-together cells. Outer cells can become contracting cells: each squeezes on its own rhythm, and a body only moves when they squeeze together. Watch the orange flashes fall into step, and compare bodies whose cells are linked with bodies whose cells each keep their own beat. Zoom right into a body to watch it squeeze.

What is real here, and what is simplified

Contraction. Cells squeeze with fibres of two proteins, actin and myosin, that slide past each other. Single cells already had them, long before animals. Muscle cells are cells that do little else.

The first muscle-like cells. In sponges, cells on the surface can contract slowly. In Hydra and jellyfish, the skin cells themselves carry contractile fibres (epitheliomuscular cells), and they are linked by gap junctions, so electrical changes spread from cell to cell. Here, a contracting cell nudges its rhythm toward the rest of its body, as strongly as its "sync" gene says. That is the Kuramoto model of coupled rhythms, the same maths as fireflies flashing together.

Why together. A body pushes water to move. Squeezes that come one after another mostly slosh the water around inside; squeezes at the same moment push it out. Here, a body's push is scaled by how in step its contracting cells are (squared), so a body in step moves about twice as fast as one out of step.

What we found. Linked bodies fall into step within a few beats and move about twice as fast as unlinked ones. Whether the linking gene itself spreads by evolution is unclear: it rose on some coasts and fell on others in our test runs, because on this coast food is often plentiful and moving faster does not always pay.

Simplified. Contracting cells sit on the outside of a body and never divide. All of a body's contracting cells push the same way (the way its oldest cell is heading). Real early animals were bigger, softer and slower. The genes are numbers, not DNA.

Two parents: the paradox of sex

Almost every animal and plant starts from one cell that mixes the genes of two parents. Yet a clone needs no partner and passes on all its genes, not half. Two coasts let you weigh the two: Clones and Two parents (add them from "Add a world", or open the guided lab "Why two parents?").

The rules. On both coasts, new bodies start only from a seed cell that a germ cell releases; daughters stay in their body and bodies do not break up. Each cell carries 10 genes that a copying mistake can break for good (5% of divisions); each broken gene makes it burn 15% more energy. On the Two parents coast, a germ cell needs a germ cell of another body within reach, and the seed takes each gene from one parent or the other, at random (free recombination). With no partner, no seed.

What theory says. Clones can only gain broken genes, one click at a time: Muller's ratchet (Muller 1964, well supported as a model). Mixing lets two parents make a seed with fewer broken genes than either, and puts good genes from different lines together (Felsenstein 1974, well supported as a model). Experiments agree: yeast that mix their genes adapt faster and shed harmful mutations (McDonald, Rice and Desai 2016, well supported).

What we found. Over five test runs of 8,000 steps, the Two parents coasts released a third to two thirds as many seeds as the Clones coasts (33 to 222, against 56 to 512): 39 to 65% of the time a ready germ cell found no partner. Broken genes per cell ended at 0.13 to 0.31 with two parents and 0.10 to 0.22 with clones; two parents ended lower on 2 coasts out of 5. Here, sex costs seeds and does not clean up the genes.

Why. The costs of sex are paid every time; its benefits need conditions. They show in small populations over many generations, against parasites that keep changing, or when many genes must change together. This coast has hundreds of cells, only a few generations of bodies and no parasites. Why sex is so common is still an open question (Otto 2009, debated). Earlier versions of this model, with bodies that age and die, crashed populations to a few bodies, and clones and two parents both piled up broken genes.

Simplified. No sexes, no egg and sperm, no meiosis: any germ cell can pair with any germ cell of another body. Genes are unlinked, and a broken gene is either broken or not.

When did this happen?

Bodies: about 1.5 billion to 600 million years ago. Small many-celled bodies appear among the first complex cells, and the oldest known muscle fibres in an animal are in Haootia, a 560-million-year-old fossil from Newfoundland (Liu et al. 2014, well supported).

Two parents: by about 1.05 billion years ago. The oldest clear fossil evidence of sex is the red alga Bangiomorpha pubescens, from the Hunting Formation on Somerset Island, Arctic Canada, with separate stages for spores and for sex cells (Butterfield 2000). Its rock was dated to 1.047 billion years ago (Gibson et al. 2018, well supported). Sex is probably older still: the last common ancestor of all complex cells already had the genes for it (Speijer, Lukeš and Eliáš 2015, well supported).
